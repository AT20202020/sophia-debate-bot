---
title: Aramaic words and wordplay in Jesus' sayings
triggers: aramaic, talitha, abba, eloi, sabachthani, ephphatha, son of man, bar enasha, wordplay, pun, idiom, idioms, casey, vermes, translation from aramaic
---
CLAIM: The Gospels, written in Greek, preserve Aramaic words and idioms, and some sayings work better translated back into Aramaic. Historicists argue this traces the sayings to an Aramaic-speaking teacher in Galilee.

CASE FOR (historicist):
- Mark keeps Aramaic phrases and translates them: "talitha koum" (5:41), "ephphatha" (7:34), "Eloi, Eloi, lema sabachthani" (15:34), and "Abba" (14:36).
- "Son of man" reflects an ordinary Aramaic idiom, which Geza Vermes (Jesus the Jew, 1973) and Maurice Casey (Aramaic Sources of Mark's Gospel, 1998) argue fits a real speaker's usage.
- Casey argues some passages read more naturally when reconstructed in Aramaic, pointing to early written or oral sources close to Jesus.

CASE AGAINST (skeptic):
- The earliest Christians in Judea spoke Aramaic regardless. Aramaic traces show the movement started among Aramaic speakers, not that the sayings came from one specific man.
- Reconstructing an Aramaic original is speculative, and Casey's reconstructions have been criticized by other scholars.
- Mark may keep Aramaic words for effect: they sound authentic or powerful, much like foreign words in healing formulas of the time.

REPLIES TO WATCH FOR: Be careful with specific "puns." Some claimed Aramaic wordplays are disputed or invented in popular apologetics; ask for the verse and the Aramaic words before accepting or rejecting one.

WHERE SCHOLARS STAND: Most scholars accept an Aramaic layer behind parts of the tradition. How much it proves about a single historical speaker is debated.

SOURCES: Mark 5:41, 7:34, 14:36, 15:34; Vermes, Jesus the Jew (1973); Casey, Aramaic Sources of Mark's Gospel (1998).
