CLAIM: The Gospels are anonymous and were written decades after the events. How late, and what kind of writing they are, decides how much history they can carry.

CASE FOR EARLY / HISTORICAL USE (historicist):
- The usual dates are Mark around 66-75 CE, Matthew and Luke around 80-90, John around 90-110, within living memory of the events.
- Burridge (What Are the Gospels?, 1992) argues they belong to the genre of Greco-Roman biography, which aimed at a real subject.
- Papias (early 2nd century) reports that Mark wrote down what Peter said.
- Anonymous doesn't mean worthless: many ancient histories circulated without a name attached.

CASE FOR LATE / LITERARY ORIGIN (skeptic/mythicist):
- The names were attached in the 2nd century; Irenaeus (around 180) is the first to name all four. Matthew and Luke copy large parts of Mark word for word, so they are not independent witnesses.
- MacDonald argues Mark models episodes on Homer, and others note heavy reworking of Old Testament stories; skeptics read this as literature, not memory.
- Klinghardt (The Oldest Gospel, 2021) and Vinzent (2014) argue Marcion's gospel came first and the canonical Gospels are later responses. Britt and Wingo (Christ Before Jesus) push all of them into the 2nd century.
- Nongbri (2005) showed the P52 fragment of John can't be dated as precisely as once claimed; anywhere from the 2nd to the early 3rd century is possible.

REPLIES TO WATCH FOR: Mark's prediction of the temple's destruction (Mark 13) is the main reason for dating it after 70; Crossley dates Mark much earlier, a minority view.

WHERE SCHOLARS STAND: The dates above are the large majority view. Marcionite priority and full 2nd-century dating are minority views.

SOURCES: Burridge, What Are the Gospels? (1992); MacDonald, The Homeric Epics and the Gospel of Mark (2000); Klinghardt, The Oldest Gospel (2021); Vinzent, Marcion and the Dating of the Synoptic Gospels (2014); Nongbri, Harvard Theological Review (2005); Eusebius, Church History 3.39 (Papias).
