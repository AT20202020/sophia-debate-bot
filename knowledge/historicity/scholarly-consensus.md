CLAIM: Most relevant scholars accept that a historical Jesus existed. The dispute is over how much weight that agreement deserves.

CASE FOR THE CONSENSUS (historicist):
- The view is held across religious lines: Jewish scholars such as Geza Vermes, the agnostic Bart Ehrman, and the atheist Gerd Ludemann all accept a historical Jesus.
- Atheist writers such as Tim O'Neill (History for Atheists) argue against mythicism. Historicity is not a theist-only position.
- Expert agreement is legitimate evidence of how specialists read the sources; relying on it is not automatically a fallacy.

CASE AGAINST LEANING ON IT (skeptic/mythicist):
- Consensus reports a judgment; it isn't itself evidence about the 1st century. Citing it in place of the sources is an appeal to authority.
- Much of the field is housed in religious institutions. Carrier and Lataster argue this shapes which questions get asked.
- Justin Meggitt of Cambridge (New Testament Studies, 2019) argues the question is legitimate and that mythicism is usually dismissed rather than examined. Philip Davies also defended asking it.
- Few scholars have published detailed peer-reviewed responses to mythicism, so the consensus may reflect the question being largely unexamined.

REPLIES TO WATCH FOR: Carrier himself concedes the consensus exists. Historicists answer that the scholars who did engage (Ehrman, Casey, Gullotta) were not persuaded.

WHERE SCHOLARS STAND: The consensus is real and broad. How much weight it should carry in a debate is what's disputed. Represent both, and don't call either side fringe or the question settled.

SOURCES: Ehrman, Did Jesus Exist? (2012); Meggitt, "'More Ingenious than Learned'?", NTS 65 (2019); Vermes, Jesus the Jew (1973); Casey, Jesus: Evidence and Argument or Mythicist Myths? (2014); O'Neill, History for Atheists (website).
