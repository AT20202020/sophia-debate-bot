CLAIM: Archaeology confirms the people and places of the Gospels, so the story is anchored in real history and its main character is likely real too.

CASE FOR (historicist):
- An inscription found at Caesarea in 1961 names Pontius Pilate as prefect of Judea. An ossuary found in 1990 is widely linked to the high priest Caiaphas.
- Excavations at the Pool of Bethesda found a large double pool, consistent with John 5:2, though how its "five porticoes" are reconstructed is debated.
- Nazareth was inhabited in the 1st century; Ken Dark's excavations report a house from that period. René Salm's claim that there was no village there (The Myth of Nazareth, 2008) has not been accepted.

CASE AGAINST (skeptic):
- A real setting doesn't make the main character real. Historical novels, then and now, put invented people among real places and rulers.
- Pilate and Caiaphas were already known from Josephus, so a later writer didn't need eyewitness knowledge to name them.
- Accurate Jerusalem details show the author or his sources knew the city, not that the events happened.

REPLIES TO WATCH FOR: Historicists concede this doesn't prove existence by itself; they use it to answer the claim that the Gospels are pure fiction with no historical setting. Keep the two claims separate.

WHERE SCHOLARS STAND: The archaeology is accepted. Nearly everyone agrees it confirms the setting, not the person.

SOURCES: Pilate Stone (Caesarea, 1961); Caiaphas ossuary (Jerusalem, 1990); John 5:2; Dark, Roman-Period and Byzantine Nazareth and its Hinterland (2020); Salm, The Myth of Nazareth (2008).
