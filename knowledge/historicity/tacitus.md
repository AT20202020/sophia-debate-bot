---
title: Tacitus, Annals 15.44
triggers: tacitus, annals, 15.44, nero, great fire, fire of rome, chrestians, chrestianos, christus, procurator
---
CLAIM: The Roman historian Tacitus (Annals 15.44, around 116 CE) says Christians took their name from "Christus," executed under Tiberius by Pontius Pilate. Historicists count this as hostile, non-Christian evidence.

CASE FOR (historicist):
- Tacitus was a careful senatorial historian with no sympathy for Christians; he calls their faith a "pernicious superstition."
- He had access to elite sources: he governed the province of Asia around 112 and was a friend of Pliny the Younger, who investigated Christians.
- The details (Tiberius, Pilate, Judea) fit what other sources say, and almost all scholars accept the passage as genuine.

CASE AGAINST (skeptic/mythicist):
- He writes about 85 years after the crucifixion. There is no evidence he checked Roman records; he may simply be repeating what Christians in his day said.
- He calls Pilate a "procurator"; an inscription found at Caesarea shows his title was "prefect." Skeptics say this shows a later, second-hand source.
- No ancient writer quotes the passage until Sulpicius Severus around 400. Carrier argues the line about Christus is an interpolation, a minority view. The earliest manuscript shows "Chrestianos" altered to "Christianos."

REPLIES TO WATCH FOR: Defenders answer that Tacitus routinely used the title of his own time, and that hostile writers rarely took Christian claims on trust. Skeptics answer that a hostile writer has no reason to dispute an origin story he does not care about.

WHERE SCHOLARS STAND: Authenticity is near-universal. The real disagreement is whether Tacitus is independent evidence or a report of Christian belief around 116, and on that scholars split.

SOURCES: Tacitus, Annals 15.44; Pliny the Younger, Letters 10.96; the Pilate Stone (Caesarea, discovered 1961); Carrier, "The Prospect of a Christian Interpolation in Tacitus," Vigiliae Christianae (2014).
