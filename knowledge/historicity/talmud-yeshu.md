---
title: Jesus in the Talmud (Yeshu)
triggers: talmud, talmudic, yeshu, sanhedrin 43a, rabbinic, rabbis, ben pandera, ben stada, eve of passover, toledot yeshu
---
CLAIM: The Babylonian Talmud says "Yeshu" was hanged on the eve of Passover for sorcery and leading Israel astray (Sanhedrin 43a). Apologists cite it as hostile Jewish testimony that Jesus existed and was executed.

CASE FOR (historicist):
- It is hostile: Jewish opponents attacked Jesus as a sorcerer and deceiver but never claimed he didn't exist.
- The Passover timing matches the Gospels. The passage is introduced as an older tradition (a baraita), which some scholars date before 200 CE.

CASE AGAINST (skeptic):
- The Talmud was compiled around 500 CE, centuries later. Peter Schafer (Jesus in the Talmud, 2007) argues these passages are deliberate polemical responses to the Gospel stories, so they depend on Christian claims rather than independent memory.
- Its details clash with the Gospels: forty days of a herald seeking defenders, hanging rather than crucifixion, five named disciples.
- Other Talmudic Yeshu passages place a Yeshu around 100 BCE, under the high priest Joshua ben Perachiah. Mythicists cite this confusion as a sign the figure was never firmly anchored in time.

REPLIES TO WATCH FOR: "Hanged" can describe crucifixion in Jewish usage (Galatians 3:13 uses the same idea). The "they never denied he existed" point also comes up with pagan critics; see the celestial-Jesus card on Celsus and Trypho.

WHERE SCHOLARS STAND: Most scholars, including those who accept a historical Jesus, treat the Talmud passages as late reactions to Christianity with little or no independent historical value.

SOURCES: Babylonian Talmud, Sanhedrin 43a, Sotah 47a; Schafer, Jesus in the Talmud (2007); Van Voorst, Jesus Outside the New Testament (2000).
