CLAIM: The Bible's God commands or approves acts nearly everyone now calls atrocities. So either those acts were good because God commanded them, or the Bible's God isn't a reliable moral standard.

CASE FOR (skeptic):
- 1 Samuel 15:3 orders the Amalekites killed, "infant and nursing child" included; Deuteronomy 20:16-17 says to leave nothing alive in Canaanite cities; Numbers 31:17-18 kills the boys and keeps the virgin girls.
- Slavery is regulated, not banned: foreigners can be bought as permanent property (Lev 25:44-46).
- Abraham is praised for being willing to kill Isaac (Gen 22; Heb 11:17). Ask a believer, "would you kill your child if God told you to?" Most say no, which suggests they judge God's commands by a standard of their own.
- On divine command theory, a jihadist's only error would be having the wrong god.

CASE AGAINST (theist):
- Paul Copan (Is God a Moral Monster?, 2011) and Copan and Flannagan (Did God Really Command Genocide?, 2014) argue "utterly destroy" is standard ancient Near Eastern war hyperbole; the same books report those peoples still living there (e.g. Judges 1).
- The commands were one-off and tied to one time and place, not general ethics. Israel's servant laws were milder than its neighbors' (Ex 21:2, 21:26-27), and theists argue revelation moves progressively toward abolition.
- Craig argues God, as the giver of life, has rights over it that humans lack. Kierkegaard (Fear and Trembling, 1843) treats Isaac as a "teleological suspension of the ethical."

REPLIES TO WATCH FOR: Skeptics answer that hyperbole still leaves commands to kill children as written, and Wes Morriston (Philosophia Christi, 2009) argues the hyperbole defense fails. "God may take life" makes right whatever God wills: the Euthyphro problem again. If they ask where her standard comes from, she doesn't need one: by Christianity's own claim God is perfectly good, so the question is whether these acts are.

ACCURACY NOTES: Whether Deut 22:28-29 describes rape or seduction is debated; say so. Don't cite "God killed 2.4 million" tallies; they're a popular count, not scholarship.

WHERE SCHOLARS STAND: Christian scholars themselves treat these texts as a serious problem; every proposed answer is contested.
