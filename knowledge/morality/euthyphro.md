---
title: The Euthyphro dilemma
triggers: euthyphro, euthyphro dilemma, because god commands, good because god, god commands it because, divine command, divine command theory, arbitrary, god's nature, god is good, robert adams, alston
---
CLAIM: From Plato's Euthyphro (10a): is something good because God commands it, or does God command it because it is good? If the first, morality is arbitrary: God could have commanded cruelty. If the second, goodness exists independently of God, and God isn't needed to ground it.

CASE FOR (skeptic):
- It targets premise 1 of the moral argument directly: either option undercuts "morality needs God."
- Scripture has God commanding acts most people now call atrocities (for example 1 Samuel 15:3). Skeptics argue a theist who says "God's command made it right" has taken the arbitrary horn.

CASE AGAINST (theist):
- It's a false dilemma, theists answer: goodness is grounded in God's NATURE, not his arbitrary will. God commands what is good because he is good, and he could not command cruelty. William Alston ("What Euthyphro Should Have Said," 1990) argues God is the standard of goodness, the way a standard measure fixes a unit.
- Robert Adams (Finite and Infinite Goods, 1999) defends a modified divine command theory: obligations come from the commands of a God who is essentially loving, so they can't be arbitrary.

REPLIES TO WATCH FOR: The skeptic reply is to move the dilemma up a level: is God's nature good because it's God's, which makes "good" mean "whatever God is like," or because it has loving, just properties we can recognize? If the latter, skeptics argue those properties are doing the moral work, not God. Wielenberg makes a version of this. Theists answer that explanation has to stop somewhere, and God is a better stopping point than brute moral facts.

WHERE SCHOLARS STAND: Live on both sides. Most philosophers of religion consider the "God's nature" reply the theist's standard answer; whether it escapes the dilemma is disputed.

SOURCES: Plato, Euthyphro 10a; Alston, "What Euthyphro Should Have Said" (1990); Adams, Finite and Infinite Goods (1999); Wielenberg, Robust Ethics (2014).
