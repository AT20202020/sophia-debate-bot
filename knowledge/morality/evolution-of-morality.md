CLAIM: Human moral behavior has natural roots: we're a social species, and cooperation, care for offspring and empathy helped our ancestors survive. Skeptics use this to explain where moral sense comes from without God.

CASE FOR (skeptic):
- Frans de Waal (Primates and Philosophers, 2006) documents empathy, fairness and reconciliation in primates. Bekoff and Pierce (Wild Justice, 2009) do the same across mammals.
- In a 2011 Science study (Bartal, Decety and Mason), rats freed trapped cagemates and often shared chocolate with them.
- Patricia Churchland (Braintrust, 2011) ties caring behavior to brain chemistry such as oxytocin.
- Curry, Mullins and Whitehouse (Current Anthropology, 2019) found the same basic cooperative morals - help family, help your group, return favors, be brave, defer, divide fairly, respect property - across 60 societies.

CASE AGAINST (theist):
- This explains moral BELIEFS, not moral TRUTH. Showing why we feel cruelty is wrong doesn't show it IS wrong.
- If morality is just evolved, theists argue it isn't objective. Michael Ruse and E.O. Wilson (1986) called ethics "an illusion fobbed off on us by our genes," and theists quote this often.
- Evolution also produced selfishness and tribal violence, so it can't by itself tell us which instincts to follow.

REPLIES TO WATCH FOR: Sophia's role fits this well: she can use it to answer "where do you get your morals?" as a description of where moral sense comes from, without claiming a theory of moral truth. If they push "so nothing is really wrong", she says plainly that cruelty is wrong and puts the burden back: they need to show God, not evolution, is what makes it wrong. Explaining a belief's origin doesn't make it false; treating it as if it does is the genetic fallacy, and that applies to both sides.

ACCURACY NOTES: The role of mirror neurons in human empathy is contested; don't present it as established.

WHERE SCHOLARS STAND: The evolutionary roots of moral behavior are widely accepted. What that means for moral truth is the open philosophical question.

SOURCES: de Waal (2006); Bekoff and Pierce (2009); Bartal, Decety and Mason, Science 334 (2011); Churchland (2011); Curry, Mullins and Whitehouse, Current Anthropology 60 (2019); Ruse and Wilson, Philosophy 61 (1986).
