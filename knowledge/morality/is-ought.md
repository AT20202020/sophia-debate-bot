title: Hume's is-ought gap

CLAIM: David Hume (A Treatise of Human Nature, 3.1.1, 1739-40) observed that writers move from statements about what IS to statements about what OUGHT to be without explaining how. Many read this as: no set of purely factual premises can logically produce a moral conclusion.

HOW THEISTS USE IT:
- Against secular ethics: you can't get morality from nature, evolution or well-being alone, because those are facts, and facts don't yield "oughts."
- G.E. Moore's related "naturalistic fallacy" (Principia Ethica, 1903) is used the same way against defining "good" as pleasure or well-being.

HOW SKEPTICS USE IT BACK:
- "God commands X" is also a statement of fact. If no "ought" follows from facts, "God commanded it, so you ought to do it" needs an extra premise ("you ought to obey God"), and that premise isn't derived from God either. The gap cuts both ways.
- So the gap doesn't favor theism; it's a general puzzle about all moral reasoning.

CASE AGAINST THE GAP ITSELF:
- John Searle ("How to Derive 'Ought' from 'Is'," 1964) argued promising does it: "I promised" (a fact) yields "I ought to" through the institution of promising. Critics say Searle sneaks the ought into the institution.
- Some naturalist realists accept the gap logically but argue moral facts can still be natural facts, the way "water is H2O" wasn't derivable by logic alone.

REPLIES TO WATCH FOR: Theists often answer that God's nature is goodness itself, so "God is good" is both an is and an ought. Skeptics reply that this is the Euthyphro question again (see that card).

WHERE SCHOLARS STAND: The gap is widely accepted as a logical point about deduction. Whether it shows anything deeper about moral facts is disputed, and it does not by itself favor theism or atheism.

SOURCES: Hume, A Treatise of Human Nature 3.1.1 (1739-40); Moore, Principia Ethica (1903); Searle, Philosophical Review 73 (1964).
