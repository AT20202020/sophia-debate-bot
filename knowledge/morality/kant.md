CLAIM: Immanuel Kant argued morality comes from reason itself. Both sides of the God debate claim him.

KANT FOR THE SKEPTIC:
- In the Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals (1785), the moral law is the categorical imperative, binding on any rational being: act only on rules you could will as universal laws, and treat people always as ends, never merely as means.
- Morality must be autonomous, self-given by reason. Acting rightly only because someone commands it, even God, is "heteronomy" and not truly moral. Skeptics use this against divine command theory.
- So, for Kant, we don't need God to know or ground our duties.

KANT FOR THE THEIST:
- In the Critique of Practical Reason (1788), Kant argues that morality aims at the "highest good": virtue rewarded with proportionate happiness. That isn't guaranteed in this life, so practical reason must POSTULATE God and immortality to make it achievable.
- Theists cite this as a major philosopher holding that morality points to God.

ACCURACY NOTES: Kant's argument is not a proof that God exists. He calls God a postulate of practical reason, a rational hope, and in the Critique of Pure Reason (1781) he rejects the traditional proofs. Don't call him an atheist or a straightforward theist; describe both sides of his view.

REPLIES TO WATCH FOR: Skeptics answer that the highest-good argument only shows we'd WANT the universe to be fair, not that it is. Kantian theists answer that a moral law with no hope of fulfillment would be irrational to follow.

WHERE SCHOLARS STAND: Kant's ethics is one of the major secular frameworks (deontology). His moral argument for God is respected but widely judged to establish at most a rational hope, as Kant himself said.

SOURCES: Kant, Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals (1785); Critique of Practical Reason (1788); Critique of Pure Reason (1781).
