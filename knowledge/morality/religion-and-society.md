CLAIM: Both sides argue from history and statistics. Theists say atheist regimes (Stalin, Mao, Pol Pot) show what happens without God. Skeptics say religion has fueled crusades, inquisitions and witch hunts, and that secular societies today are among the most peaceful.

CASE FOR "SECULAR IS FINE" (skeptic):
- Phil Zuckerman (Society Without God, 2008) describes Denmark and Sweden as highly secular and among the world's safest, healthiest societies.
- Gregory Paul (Journal of Religion and Society, 2005 and 2009) reports that among developed nations, higher religiosity correlates with more social problems, though his methods have been criticized.
- The Soviet and Maoist regimes were driven by Marxist-Leninist ideology and state power; skeptics argue atheism itself was not the motive.

CASE FOR "RELIGION HELPS" (theist):
- Arthur Brooks (Who Really Cares, 2006) found religious Americans give more to charity, including secular charity. Putnam and Campbell (American Grace, 2010) found them more civically engaged, though they credit religious community rather than belief.
- The communist regimes persecuted religion openly and on purpose.
- The Scandinavian countries were shaped by centuries of Christian culture, so theists argue they are living off that inheritance.

REPLIES TO WATCH FOR: Correlation isn't causation in either direction: wealth, inequality and stable government explain much of the pattern. Both sides tend to count their opponents' worst and their own best.

ACCURACY NOTES: "93% of scientists are atheists" is wrong. That figure (Larson and Witham, Nature, 1998) is for elite National Academy of Sciences members; a 2009 Pew survey found about a third of US scientists believe in God. Popular claims that prisons are full of Christians and nearly empty of atheists come from poorly sourced figures; don't use them.

WHERE SCHOLARS STAND: There's no consensus that religion makes societies more or less moral overall; the data are mixed and heavily confounded.

SOURCES: Zuckerman (2008); Paul, J. Religion & Society (2005, 2009); Brooks (2006); Putnam and Campbell (2010); Larson and Witham, Nature 394 (1998); Pew Research Center, "Scientists and Belief" (2009).
