CLAIM: Christians today don't stone adulterers (Lev 20:10) or Sabbath-breakers (Ex 31:14-15), don't avoid mixed fabrics (Lev 19:19), and reject slavery. Skeptics argue they're choosing which commands to follow by a moral sense that doesn't come from scripture, which shows morality isn't actually derived from God's commands.

CASE FOR (skeptic):
- Believers routinely override texts they find cruel, and they agree on which ones by using the same moral sense nonbelievers use.
- Jesus says he came not to abolish the law and that not "one jot or one tittle" will pass from it (Matt 5:17-18).
- Christians argued both for and against slavery from the same Bible; the historian Mark Noll (The Civil War as a Theological Crisis, 2006) documents this. The text alone didn't settle it.

CASE AGAINST (theist):
- A long tradition divides Old Testament law into moral, civil and ceremonial parts (Aquinas, Summa Theologiae I-II q.99; the Westminster Confession, ch. 19). The civil and ceremonial laws governed ancient Israel as a nation and are fulfilled in Christ (Heb 8:13; Acts 10; Gal 3:24-25); the moral law still binds.
- Following the new covenant isn't cherry-picking; it's how Christianity has always read the Old Testament.
- Jewish tradition also didn't apply these penalties literally: the Mishnah calls a court that executes once in seven years "destructive" (Makkot 1:10).

REPLIES TO WATCH FOR: Skeptics answer that the three-way division isn't stated in the text itself, so deciding which law goes in which category takes an independent moral judgment, which is the point. Theists answer that the New Testament itself sets aside food and ritual laws (Mark 7:19; Acts 10).

WHERE SCHOLARS STAND: The moral/civil/ceremonial division is standard in Christian theology. Biblical scholars debate whether it fits the texts.

SOURCES: Lev 19:19, 20:10; Ex 31:14-15; Matt 5:17-18; Heb 8:13; Gal 3:24-25; Mark 7:19; Aquinas, ST I-II q.99; Westminster Confession ch. 19; Mishnah Makkot 1:10; Noll (2006).
